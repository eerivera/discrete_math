# Skill Description

Combine products, combinations, disjoint cases, and subtraction to count outcomes satisfying several conditions. Explain exclusions and any symmetry factors.

---

# Sample Problems

Explain how each choice determines an outcome and why the outcome is counted once. Give a numerical answer for the dice problem; an exact expression is sufficient for the card problem.

For the card problem, let $R=\lbrace 1,2,\ldots,10\rbrace$ be the set of ten ranks and $S=\lbrace \mathrm{hearts},\mathrm{diamonds},\mathrm{clubs},\mathrm{spades}\rbrace$ the set of four suits. The deck is $D=R\times S$, with one copy of each rank-suit card, for 40 distinct cards. A hand is an unordered set of distinct cards, drawn without replacement. A pair means two cards of the same rank.

## Practice Exam Problem 1

How many five-card hands have exactly one pair: two cards of one rank and three other cards whose ranks are all distinct and different from the pair's rank? This excludes two pairs, three or four cards of one rank, and a full house (three of one rank and two of another).

## Practice Exam Problem 2

Five distinguishable eight-sided dice each show an integer from 1 through 8. How many rolls have three dice showing one value and two dice showing a different value?

# Tutorial

## Define a representation

Identify exactly what the outcome records. A card is a rank-suit pair, so two cards can have the same rank while remaining distinct. A card hand is a set; a roll of distinguishable dice assigns a value to each labeled position. These representations determine which changes produce different outcomes.

For an exact-pattern problem, describe the allowed repetitions before choosing objects. Exactly one pair has rank multiplicities $2+1+1+1$: one rank occurs twice and three other ranks occur once each. A full-house roll has value multiplicities $3+2$, with different values for the two groups.

## Design a construction

Describe choices that generate the outcome. At each step, state what has already been chosen, what remains available, and whether the choice is ordered. Combine [F18's products and cases](F18.md) with [F19's ordered selections and subsets](F19.md).

When grouping cards by rank, a repeated rank needs a subset of its available suits. Singleton ranks must satisfy the required distinctness conditions. When grouping dice by value, choose which **labeled positions** receive the repeated value; their internal order does not create extra choices.

The identities of later choices can depend on earlier choices. Check that the number of extensions is the claimed count for every valid earlier choice before multiplying. If it varies, split into disjoint cases.

## Check exclusions and overcounting

Use three checks to justify a construction:

1. Every generated outcome satisfies the conditions. In an exactly-one-pair hand, the singleton ranks must differ from one another and from the pair's rank.
2. Every valid outcome can be generated. Starting from an arbitrary finished outcome, identify the choices that would reconstruct it.
3. Each outcome has exactly one construction, or a known constant number of constructions. If two groups have different sizes, those sizes distinguish the groups. Equal-sized groups may need an ordering convention or a symmetry factor.

If you subtract forbidden outcomes, verify that they are a subset of the initial count and are counted once each. Explicit conditions determine whether a category is included: for example, all-one-suit hands may be allowed in one problem and excluded in another.

These checks are also useful when designing an algorithm that generates outcomes: an omitted case loses valid outputs, and multiple construction paths can produce duplicates. The explanation of why the construction is complete and unique is part of the solution.

For more applications, use the [card-counting notes](../../notes/counting/permsAndCombs.md#counting-cards) and [named-group partitions](../../notes/counting/permsAndCombs.md#multinomial-theorem).

# Answers

## Practice Exam Problem 1

Choose the pair's rank, its two suits, three distinct other ranks, and their suits:

$$
10\binom{4}{2}\binom{9}{3}4^3=10\cdot6\cdot84\cdot64=322,560.
$$

The three singleton ranks are all different and exclude the pair's rank, enforcing exactly one pair. The pair is unique in every counted hand.

## Practice Exam Problem 2

Choose the triple's value in 8 ways, the pair's value in 7 ways, and the triple's labeled positions in $\binom{5}{3}$ ways. Thus

$$
8\cdot7\binom{5}{3}=8\cdot7\cdot10=560.
$$

Each roll has a unique triple and a unique pair of a different value.
