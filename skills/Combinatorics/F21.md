# Skill Description

Use finite domain and codomain sizes to prove collisions, bounds on fibers, and impossibility of injections, surjections, or bijections. Define the function used in a counting proof.

---

# Sample Problems

All functions below are total and all sets are finite. Prove each claim for every set satisfying its hypotheses.

## Practice Exam Problem 1

Let $S$ be any set of 11 distinct integers from 1 through 150. Prove that two distinct subsets of $S$ have the same sum.

## Practice Exam Problem 2

Let $|A|=a$ and $|B|=b$, and let $f:A^2\rightarrow B^3$.

1. If $f$ is a bijection and $b=9$, find $a$ and justify it.
2. Prove that $f$ cannot be a bijection when $b=2$.

## Practice Exam Problem 3

A function assigns each of 65 jobs to one of 9 workers. Prove that at least one worker receives at least 8 jobs.

# Tutorial

## Compare finite sizes

Think of inputs as pigeons and possible outputs as holes. Injectivity means each hole receives at most one pigeon. Surjectivity means each hole receives at least one. For a total function $f:A\rightarrow B$ between finite sets,

| Property | Necessary size condition |
| --- | --- |
| Injective | $\vert A\vert\le\vert B\vert$ |
| Surjective | $\vert A\vert\ge\vert B\vert$ |
| Bijective | $\vert A\vert=\vert B\vert$ |

**Proof.** The image has at most $|A|$ elements and is contained in $B$. Injectivity makes $f$ a bijection from $A$ to its image; surjectivity makes its image all of $B$. Combining the two gives the bijection condition. **Q.E.D.**

These are necessary conditions on the given function. Equal sizes alone do not prove that a particular function is bijective. On finite sets of equal size an injective total function is surjective and a surjective total function is injective, since its image then must have the full codomain size. The finite-size argument does not extend to arbitrary infinite sets.

**Pigeonhole principle.** If $|A|>|B|$, every total function $A\rightarrow B$ maps two distinct inputs to the same output.

**Proof.** An injection would give $|A|\le|B|$, a contradiction. **Q.E.D.**

## Fibers and larger collisions

The fiber $f^{-1}(\lbrace b\rbrace)=\lbrace a\in A:f(a)=b\rbrace$ is the set of inputs reaching $b$. It does not require an inverse function. The fibers are disjoint and cover $A$, so

$$
|A|=\sum_{b\in B}|f^{-1}(\lbrace b\rbrace)|.
$$

If $|A|>k|B|$ for an integer $k\ge0$, some fiber has at least $k+1$ elements. Otherwise every fiber would have size at most $k$, giving $|A|\le k|B|$, a contradiction. For nonempty $B$, some fiber has at least $\lceil|A|/|B|\rceil$ elements. To guarantee at least $t$ in one fiber, show $|A|>(t-1)|B|$.

## Build a subset-sum proof

Specify the domain: all subsets give $2^{|S|}$ inputs; subsets of a fixed size $r$ give $\binom{|S|}{r}$. Define $h(U)=\sum_{u\in U}u$ and give a finite **integer** codomain containing every possible sum. All subsets include $\emptyset$, with sum 0. If $S$ has $n$ positive elements at most $M$, the integer set $\lbrace 0,1,\ldots,nM\rbrace$ suffices and has $nM+1$ elements. For fixed $r\ge1$, $\lbrace r,r+1,\ldots,rM\rbrace$ suffices and has $rM-r+1$ elements.

A bound may be loose. You do not need every integer in the chosen codomain to occur. Once the domain is larger, conclude that distinct subsets give the same sum. A single numerical example is not a proof for every allowed $S$.

## Power sets and logarithms

If $|S|=n$, then $|\mathcal P(S)|=2^n$ by the indicator-sequence bijection: each element is either included or omitted. For every integer $n\ge0$, $n<2^n$.

**Proof of the inequality.** At $n=0$ and $n=1$, it reads $0<1$ and $1<2$. For $n\ge1$, if $n<2^n$, then $n+1\le2n<2^{n+1}$. Induction proves the claim. **Q.E.D.**

Consequently no total function $S\rightarrow\mathcal P(S)$ is surjective: a surjection would require $n\ge2^n$. This proof includes the empty set, whose power set has one element.

There is also a direct proof that works for infinite sets. For $f:S\rightarrow\mathcal P(S)$, define

$$
U=\lbrace s\in S:s\notin f(s)\rbrace.
$$

If $U=f(t)$ for some $t\in S$, then $t\in U$ implies $t\notin f(t)=U$, and $t\notin U$ implies $t\in f(t)=U$ by the definition of $U$. Both cases contradict themselves. Thus $U$ is not reached and $f$ is not surjective. This diagonal proof gives another explanation of the power-set obstruction.

For cardinalities, count products and powers before comparing: $|A^r|=a^r$ and $|\mathcal P(B)|=2^b$. Apply $\log_2$ only to positive quantities. It is increasing, so it preserves inequalities, and $\log_2(a^r)=r\log_2(a)$ for $a>0$. Empty-domain cases must be considered before taking logarithms.

## Pairing and threshold arguments

To force a pair summing to $2m+1$ among numbers $1,\ldots,2m$, group the numbers into the $m$ disjoint pairs $\lbrace i,2m+1-i\rbrace$, for $1\le i\le m$. Choosing $m+1$ numbers forces both members of some pair. State the holes and explain why every input belongs to exactly one.

A counting proof often establishes existence without locating the objects. Defining the function, proving that it is well-defined, and comparing its domain and codomain are the central reasoning steps. The [additional notes](../../notes/counting/pigeonhole.md) include the 20-integer example and proofs comparing a set with its square.

# Answers

## Practice Exam Problem 1

Define $h:\mathcal P(S)\rightarrow\lbrace 0,1,\ldots,1650\rbrace$ by $h(U)=\sum_{u\in U}u$. The sum is at most $11\cdot150=1650$, and the empty subset gives 0. The domain has $2^{11}=2048$ elements, while the codomain has 1651. Hence $h$ is not injective, giving distinct subsets with equal sums. **Q.E.D.**

## Practice Exam Problem 2

1. A bijection requires $a^2=b^3$. With $b=9$, this is $a^2=729$, so $a=27$; cardinalities are nonnegative.
2. With $b=2$, a bijection would require $a^2=8$, which is impossible for an integer $a$ since $2^2<8<3^2$. **Q.E.D.**

## Practice Exam Problem 3

If every worker received at most 7 jobs, at most $9\cdot7=63$ jobs would be assigned. But all 65 jobs are assigned. This contradiction proves that some worker receives at least 8 jobs. Equivalently, $\lceil65/9\rceil=8$. **Q.E.D.**
