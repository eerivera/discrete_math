# Skill Description

Count finite sets by constructing outcomes through successive choices and splitting them into disjoint cases. Explain why each outcome is counted exactly once.

---

# Sample Problems

Explain where you use addition and multiplication. Repetition is allowed unless stated otherwise. An exact expression is sufficient.

## Practice Exam Problem 1

A label has one of the forms LLDDDD, DDDLLL, or LLDDLL, where L is one of 26 uppercase letters and D is one of 10 digits. Initial zeros are allowed. How many labels are there?

## Practice Exam Problem 2

A game records the type of a spinner and the number it shows. There are three types, with 4, 7, and 9 numbered sectors respectively; each sector on a spinner has a different number. Two successive spins are recorded in order, and either spin may use any type. How many records are possible? Types are recorded even when their numbers agree.

# Tutorial

## The multiplication principle

For finite sets,

$$
|A\times B|=|A||B|,\qquad |A_1\times\cdots\times A_n|=\prod_{i=1}^n|A_i|.
$$

**Proof.** For every first coordinate there are $|B|$ choices of second coordinate. The rows of pairs are disjoint, giving $|A||B|$. Repeating this argument proves the formula for more factors. **Q.E.D.**

More generally, if every valid choice of the first $i-1$ entries has exactly $r_i$ possible next entries, and each outcome has exactly one construction, the total is $r_1\cdots r_n$. The identities of later choices may depend on earlier choices; their counts must be the stated ones for every valid prefix. If the counts vary, split into cases before multiplying.

For a set $A$ with $m$ elements, there are $m^n$ sequences in $A^n$. There is one empty sequence when $n=0$.

## The addition principle

If finite sets $A_1,\ldots,A_r$ are pairwise disjoint, then

$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^r A_i\right|=\sum_{i=1}^r|A_i|.
$$

**Proof.** Each element belongs to exactly one case, so adding the case sizes counts it once. **Q.E.D.**

Check disjointness before adding. Cases with different lengths are disjoint. Patterns that require a digit in one position and a letter in the same position are disjoint. If cases overlap, use [F20](F20.md).

## Explaining a count

Identify the outcome, the choices that specify it, and the number of options at each step. When adding cases, explain why they are disjoint and cover all valid outcomes. When a recorded outcome includes a type, keep that type: a 2 on one kind of spinner differs from a 2 on another kind.

A repeated choice differs from a repeated set listing: tuples and strings may repeat entries. A set lists each distinct element once. Use a falling product rather than a power when earlier choices cannot be reused; see [F19](F19.md).

Model a recorded outcome as a tuple whose coordinates are exactly the information kept. A good construction gives a bijection between valid choice paths and outcomes; this explains why the count is correct. The [additional notes](../../notes/counting/basicCounting.md) contain binary strings, plates, passwords, and leading-zero examples.

# Answers

## Practice Exam Problem 1

The three patterns have respectively $26^2\cdot10^4$, $10^3\cdot26^3$, and $26^4\cdot10^2$ possibilities. The first pattern differs from the second in its first position, and from the third in its fifth position; the second and third differ in their first position. Thus the patterns are disjoint and the answer is

$$
26^2\cdot10^4+10^3\cdot26^3+26^4\cdot10^2.
$$

## Practice Exam Problem 2

One recorded spin has $4+7+9=20$ possibilities, since the recorded types distinguish the three cases. Each first record can be followed by any second record. Thus there are $(4+7+9)^2=400$ ordered records. Equivalently, sum the nine products for the ordered pairs of spinner types.
