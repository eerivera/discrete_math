# Skill Description

Ability to write a well-structured proof of a Theorem, Proposition, Lemma or Corollary using a proof by contradiction. Assume the negation of the claim, derive incompatible statements, and explain why the contradiction proves the claim.

---

# Sample Problems

## Problem 1
Prove the following using a proof by contradiction.

**Theorem.** The cube root of 11 is irrational.

You may use the fact that if a prime $p$ divides $u^k$, where $u$ is an integer and $k$ is a positive integer, then $p$ divides $u$.

# Tutorial

To prove a statement $C$ by contradiction, assume $\neg C$ and derive an impossibility. For an implication, retain the hypothesis and negate the conclusion. State exactly which two claims contradict one another; an unexpected equation alone is not enough.

For example, the negation of $\forall x . P(x)$ is $\exists x . \neg P(x)$: a contradiction proof may begin with a hypothetical counterexample.

To prove that $A \rightarrow B$, assume $A$ is True but $B$ is False and show this generates a contradiction and hence can't be True.  Thus whenever $A$ is True, $B$ can't be False, so $B$ must also be True.

Let's use this to prove that $n^2$ is odd implies $n$ is odd.

**Proof:**
Suppose that $n^2$ is odd, but $n$ is not odd. Then $n$ must be even.
<br>
So $n=2k$ for some integer $k$, so $n^2 = (2k)^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is a multiple of 2 and hence is even.
<br>
This contradicts our premise that $n^2$ is odd, so $n$ can not be even, so it must be odd.
<br>
**QED**

## Interesting Application.
Let's use these techniques to prove something more interesting, that the square root of 2 is irrational, that is, can't be expressed as a fraction $a/b$ where $a$ and $b$ are integers.

**Theorem**  $\sqrt{2}$ is an irrational number.

**Proof**
We will prove this by contradiction. Assume $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ for positive integers $a$ and $b$, with $b\ne 0$.

Divide $a$ and $b$ by their greatest common divisor to put the fraction in lowest terms. This preserves its value and makes $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

Thus they cannot both be even, since otherwise 2 would be a common divisor.

so $\sqrt{2}=a/b$ iff $2 = (a/b)^2$ iff $2=a^2/b^2$ iff $2b^2 = a^2$. So $a^2$ is even, and hence $a$ must be even by the square-parity result proved in [F08](F08.md): an integer has an even square if and only if it is even.

Hence $a=2k$ for some integer $k$.

Thus $2b^2=a^2$ iff $2b^2=(2k)^2$ iff $2b^2 = 4k^2$ iff $b^2=2k^2$ by factoring 2 out of both sides.

So $b^2$ is even, hence by our results above $b$ must be even.

But we chose $a$, $b$ so that $a/b$ was in lowest terms and hence they can't both be divisible by $2$.

This contradiction shows that $\sqrt{2}$ can not be a rational number, hence it is irrational.

**QED**

# Answers

## Problem 1
Prove the following using a proof by contradiction.

**Theorem.** The cube root of 11 is irrational.

Use the prime-divisibility fact supplied in the problem.

**Proof:**

We will prove this by contradiction. Let's assume that the cube root of 11 is not irrational,
then there exist positive integers $m$ and $n$, with $n\ne 0$, such that $11^{1/3}=m/n$ and the fraction $m/n$ is in lowest terms,
that is $m$ and $n$ have no common divisor greater than 1.

Let's cube both sides of the equation so we get

$11 = m^3/n^3$

and multiplying both sides by $n^3$ we get

$11 n^3 = m^3$

which implies that 11 divides $m^3$ and so 11 must divide $m$.

So $m = 11k$ for some integer $k$ and this implies that

$11 n^3 = (11 k)^3$

so

$n^3 = 11* 11 * k^3$

and so 11 divides $n^3$ and hence 11 divides $n$ and this shows that 11 divides $n$ and $m$
but we assumed that $n$ and $m$ had no common divisor greater than 1.

This contradiction shows that the cube root of 11 can not be rational.
**Q.E.D.**
